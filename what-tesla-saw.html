His law · n = 1/(2π√(LC)) · the Notes, 3 July 1899

Twice the tone takes a quarter of the capacity

Every circuit has "a period of vibration of its own analogous to the period of vibration of a weighted spring" (568,178). On 3 July 1899 his secondary rang at "n = 20,700 per sec. approx." with 15 jars a side. The ground waves wanted 48 jars a side, more than the transformer could charge, and "An octave would require only 12 jars on each side ... the octave vibration had only 1/4 of the energy." Move the jars and watch his two laws do the arithmetic.

Same coil throughout; the tone scales as 1/√(jars), and at one voltage the stored energy ½CV² scales with the jars.

His law · m = pL/R · 462,418, 685,012, the Notes

The magnifying factor is the tone over twice its decay

His rule: the rise is "directly proportionate to" inductance "and inversely to" resistance. On 2 January 1900 his extra coil had p = 585,000 and L = 0.018, so m = 585,000 × 0.018 ÷ 1 = 10,530, "falling to about 10" with the lamps' 1,000 ohms. Written as a complex tone ω = √(p² − γ²) − iγ with γ = R/2L, the same number is m = p/2γ: how many swings it rings before it fades.

His measured coils, November 1899

Two circuits tuned alike, coupled tight, ring at two tones

He measured every part: secondary 9,023,000 cm and 1.382 ohm, primary 230,945 cm, mutual inductance 851,021 and 863,313 cm, secondary capacity 622 cm. Tuning the primary by his rule L₁C₁ = L₂C₂ takes 30 of his 0.0009 mfd bottles, and each circuit alone rings at 63,700 per second. Coupled at k = 0.59 they ring at about 50,500 and 100,000 per second, the two numbers he published in 1900.

The exact solution with losses: (z² + a₁z + 1)(z² + a₂z + 1) = k²z⁴, a = R/(Lω₀).

His law · 593,138 (1897), 787,412, the Notes

Zero at one end, maximum at the other

A grounded coil about a quarter wave long: "at one terminal the potential would be zero and at the other maximum." Such a wire rings only at 1, 3, 5, 7 ... times its lowest tone. His own wire lengths and tones, put through speed = 4 × length × frequency, give the speed each wire would need, against his 186,000 miles per second.

One geometry: across at the speed of light, around at π/2 times it

His figures: a wave train of "0.08484 of a second" at "about four hundred and seventy-one thousand two hundred and forty kilometers per second" over the surface, the earth's diameter "an odd multiple of the quarter wave length", and "The lowest frequency would appear to be six per second." With π = 3.1416, c = 300,000 km/s and D = 12,726 km they are one picture.

The Notes, the 1904 article, and 787,412 together

A pattern pinned to the storm sweeps past a still detector

"It showed clearly the existence of stationary waves ... the point where the lightning struck the ground would be a nodal point." His detector restarted about every half hour as the storm drew off to about 300 km, and his patent gives the earth waves as 25 to 70 km long. On his nodal reading each restart is the storm moving on half a wave.

The wave is the same either way; only the storm's speed and the count of restarts change.

The Notes, 23 October and 11 November 1899

The rise is the wire it was threaded on

A ball alone loses capacity as it rises off the ground. His readings rose as he slid it up the vertical wire. Rebuilt from his coil, wire and ball, the circuit gives the rise: low down the coil top and the wire above crowd the ball; at the free end of the wire it takes the end's charge.

His own doubt: the fundamental or the first octave

For the iron-pipe structure he computed 464.8 cm from its parts. By resonance he read 339.7, 311.2, 310.6, 304.7, 305.0 and, with a new coil, 267.5 cm; with the rounded hood about 358 cm. On 26 November: "It now seems that the tuning ... was made to the first octave instead of to the fundamental tone. This is to be ascertained."

By his own law, tuning to the octave means the true capacity is four times the value read.

One more jet in one set than the other

His mercury break: jets of 9 and of 10 give "ninety makes and breaks" a turn. His rule, "one more nozzle in one set than in the other", makes every meeting a separate instant. Counts that share a factor meet fewer times: 8 and 10 give only 40.

Patents 1,061,206 (1913) and 1,209,359 (1916)

One third or one half: which drag law

The turbine patent says the torque goes as the square of the fluid's relative speed, and that the work is greatest at half the fluid's speed. With work = torque × runner speed, the square law peaks at one third. Half is what his later law gives: drag "exactly proportionate to its velocity relative to the system" in a narrow gap.
